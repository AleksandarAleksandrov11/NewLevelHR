"Quick feedback" is rarely quick and even more rarely just feedback. We've collected the five most common variants and decoded them for you, so you know what to expect in your next 1:1.

## The five translations

### 1. "Just a quick thing…"

**What it means:** There is a list. The quick thing is item one of six, and item four is the real reason for the conversation.

**What to do:** Ask, kindly, "Is there anything else on your list? I would rather hear everything now." Managers are relieved when someone opens the door for them.

### 2. "I've had some thoughts…"

**What it means:** Someone else had the thoughts. Your manager is relaying feedback from a peer, a client or their own boss, and has not yet decided how much they agree with it.

**What to do:** Ask where the feedback comes from and what specifically was observed. You cannot act on a mood; you can act on an example.

### 3. "Can we do a quick sync?"

**What it means:** A decision has already been made. The sync is where you find out about it.

**What to do:** Do not argue the decision in the sync; ask what it means for your priorities this week and what you should stop doing. Then ask when decisions like this could involve you earlier.

### 4. "Some quick feedback on the deck…"

**What it means:** The deck is fine. The direction is not. Slide formatting is the safest place to start a conversation about strategy.

**What to do:** Ask "Is it the slides or the story?" You will save both of you an afternoon of font changes.

### 5. "No big deal, but…"

**What it means:** It is a big deal, and it has been one for a while. The softener is there to protect the manager, not you.

**What to do:** Take it seriously without taking it dramatically. Repeat what you heard, agree on one concrete change and a date to check in.

## Why managers talk like this

Almost never out of malice. The hard part of feedback is not the message, it is the discomfort of delivering it. "Quick" shrinks the conversation in the manager's head; it does not shrink the problem. Most of the managers we coach know exactly what they want to say. They just want it to be over before it starts.

The cost lands on the other side of the table. Employees spend the evening decoding a sentence that took four seconds to say.

## Feedback that needs no translation

If you lead people, here is the short version of what we teach in coaching:

- **Say what it is about in the invitation.** "Feedback on the client call from Tuesday" is kinder than "quick chat?", even if it sounds harder.
- **Describe behaviour and impact, not character.** "The proposal went out without the pricing table, and the client asked twice" beats "you need to be more careful".
- **One topic per conversation.** If there are six things, you have a performance conversation, not a quick one. Plan it as such.
- **Ask before you tell.** "How do you think the call went?" often gets you most of the way there, with less defensiveness.
- **Agree on the next step and a date.** Feedback without a follow-up is just an opinion.
- **Do it soon, in private, and in person or on a call.** Never as a comment thread at 18:47 on a Friday.

> **Takeaway:** "Quick feedback" is a symptom of a manager who cares but has not been shown how to have the conversation. The fix is not thicker skin on the receiving end. It is clearer words on the giving end, and a bit of practice.
