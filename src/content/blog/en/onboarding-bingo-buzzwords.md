Synergies, alignment, deep dive, touchpoint: the first day at work is often a buzzword bingo. We put together a playful card so new colleagues can celebrate surviving their first day.

## How to play

The rules are simple, and they are the only rules on day one that nobody will explain twice:

1. Print the card or keep it open on a second screen. Discreetly.
2. Every time someone says one of the phrases in a meeting, an email or the welcome chat, cross it off.
3. Four in a row is a bingo. A full card means you have met the whole leadership team.

There is no prize. Surviving is the prize.

### The card

| | | | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Synergies | Let's take this offline | Deep dive | Touchpoint |
| Alignment | Circle back | Low-hanging fruit | Bandwidth |
| Move the needle | Quick win | Ping me | Best practice |
| Going forward | North star | Ownership | Let's park that |

Bonus square, worth two points: someone says "we are like a family here" before lunch.

## Why day one sounds like this

Nobody does it on purpose. Jargon is shorthand, and shorthand is useful between people who share the same context. The problem is that a new colleague does not share that context yet. On their first day they hear "let's align on the touchpoints going forward" and quietly wonder whether a touchpoint is a meeting, a document or a person.

The result is a strange first week: everyone is friendly, everyone is busy, and the new hire nods a lot. Nodding is not understanding. It is politeness with a deadline.

## What good onboarding language sounds like

Now the serious part, briefly. The language you use in the first days sets the tone for how safe it is to ask questions later. A few habits make a real difference:

- **Say the plain version first.** "We need to agree on who talks to the customer and when" beats "let's align on customer touchpoints" every single time.
- **Name things by their names.** Tools, teams, rituals and documents all have real names. Use them, and add one sentence about what each one is for.
- **Explain every acronym once, in writing.** A one-page glossary of internal terms is the cheapest onboarding document you will ever create.
- **Replace "ownership" with a sentence.** Who decides, who does the work, and who needs to know. Three names, no abstraction.
- **Say "I don't know yet" out loud.** When a manager admits an open question, the new hire learns that open questions are allowed.

## A quick self-check for managers

Before the next first day, read your welcome email and your day-one agenda and ask yourself:

1. Would a smart person from a different industry understand every sentence?
2. Does the new hire know what they should actually do this week, in verbs?
3. Is there one named person they can ask anything, without booking a slot?
4. Have you told them what "good" looks like at the end of month one?

If you answer "not really" to two of these, the bingo card will be full by Wednesday.

> **Takeaway:** Play the game, laugh about it, then fix the language. Clear words on day one are not a style choice. They are the first sign a new colleague gets of whether this is a place where it is safe to ask.
