We receive a lot of applications, but this one stuck with us: perfectly formatted, flawlessly written, and with a single, gloriously out-of-place meme at the end of the cover letter. Why we said yes anyway (or maybe because of it).

## What the meme actually told us

Let's be precise: nobody got a job because of a meme. The meme was interesting because of everything around it. The CV was clean. The cover letter answered the job ad point by point, in the order the ad had asked. The candidate had clearly read what the company does and what the role needed. And then, at the very end, one image, one line, perfectly aimed at a frustration anyone in that role would recognise.

That combination sends three signals at once:

- **Judgement of tone.** The candidate had guessed, correctly, how formal the company was and how much room there was to be human.
- **Self-awareness.** It was one meme, at the end, after the serious work was done. Not a personality show, just a wink.
- **Understanding of the job.** The joke only worked if you knew the daily reality of the role. That is domain knowledge in disguise.

## The personality signals that matter in an application

Recruiters talk a lot about "personality", and usually they mean "I liked them". That is not a criterion. These are the signals that actually predict how someone will work with you:

- **How they describe past work.** "We shipped" versus "I built": both are fine, but the mix tells you how they see their contribution and their team.
- **Specificity.** Real examples with real constraints ("a two-person team, six weeks, one legacy system") beat adjectives ("results-driven, passionate") every time.
- **Evidence they read the ad.** An application that mirrors your priorities shows attention. One that could have gone to any company shows volume.
- **How they handle a mistake.** A typo is noise. A sentence like "I underestimated X and here is what I changed" is signal.
- **Tone match.** Not the same personality as your team, but a tone that would not feel out of place in your Slack.

## When personality helps, and when it hurts

### It helps when

- the role is customer-facing and tone is part of the job;
- the team is small and everyone works closely together;
- the candidate uses it sparingly, after the substance is covered;
- it reveals something relevant, like humour under pressure.

### It hurts when

- it replaces substance instead of following it;
- it is aimed at the reader rather than at the work;
- it ignores the obvious register of the company;
- it makes you decide on likeability before you have checked the skills.

The honest test: would the application still be strong with the meme removed? Ours was. That is why the meme was a plus and not a red flag.

## Keep it fair: structured screening

A charming application is exactly the moment when screening gets sloppy. Personality should be a tiebreaker, never a criterion. A few rules keep the process fair for everyone in the pile:

1. **Write the criteria before you open the first application.** Three to five must-haves, in plain words.
2. **Score every application against the same list,** ideally by two people, before you compare notes.
3. **Separate "can do the job" from "I would like to work with them".** Both matter; only the first belongs in the screening column.
4. **Ask the same interview questions in the same order.** Structured interviews are more predictive and easier to defend.
5. **Notice the halo.** If you find yourself excusing a gap because you liked the tone, write the gap down anyway.

> **Takeaway:** Humour is not a competency. But judgement, self-awareness and an understanding of the job are, and sometimes they show up as one well-placed meme. Hire for the first three. Enjoy the fourth.
